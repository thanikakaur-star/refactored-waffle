Periods & cycle

"Normal" varies a lot — most cycles run 21–40 days with 2–7 days of bleeding. What matters is a change from your normal, and whether it's affecting your life. Painful or heavy periods are common, but they are not something you simply have to put up with.

Worth getting checked

  • Flooding, clots, or soaking through pads/tampons every hour or two
  • Pain that stops you working, sleeping or going to school
  • Bleeding between periods or after sex
  • Periods that stop, or become very irregular
  • Feeling exhausted or breathless — ask for an iron/anaemia check

You deserve answers

Heavy bleeding, endometriosis and PCOS are often brushed off or take years to diagnose. Track your cycle, pain and bleeding, and ask clearly for investigation or referral if you're not being heard.

Women's health

Conditions that are common, treatable, and too often dismissed. Knowing the name of what you might be dealing with helps you ask for the right assessment.

What this covers

  • Endometriosis & chronic pelvic pain
  • PCOS & cycle irregularities
  • Fibroids & heavy menstrual bleeding
  • Recurrent UTIs & bladder comfort
  • Gynaecology / fertility pathways

Getting taken seriously

Bring a few months of symptom notes and a clear "this is how it affects my life." If you're declined, it's reasonable to ask for the reason to be recorded, and to use Right to Choose for a gynaecology referral.

Babies & newborns

Your health visitor and your baby's red book (personal child health record) are there for you between GP visits. Trust your instincts — you know your baby best.

Good to know

  • Keep feeding, nappies and temperature noted — patterns help clinicians
  • Follow safer-sleep guidance to reduce the risk of SIDS
  • Keep up with the NHS vaccination schedule and development reviews
  • It's always okay to ask your health visitor or ring 111 for reassurance

When to get urgent help

Call 999 for a baby who is hard to wake, has trouble breathing, a rash that doesn't fade under a glass, a fit/seizure, or a high-pitched or weak cry. Trust your gut — you will not be wasting anyone's time.

Pregnancy & maternal health equity

You can usually self-refer to maternity services as soon as you know you're pregnant — you don't have to wait for a GP. Booking by 10 weeks means earlier checks and support.

Know the red flags — speak up straight away

  • A change in your baby's usual movements (don't wait — ring your maternity unit any time, day or night)
  • A bad headache, vision changes or sudden swelling (possible pre-eclampsia)
  • Bleeding, severe pain, or feeling feverish and unwell
  • A strong sense that something is wrong — say so clearly and ask to be checked

Honest words on inequality

Maternity care should be safe for everyone, of every background. But UK data (MBRRACE-UK) shows Black women have been around two to three times more likely to die in pregnancy or soon after than white women, with raised risks for some Asian and ethnic-minority women and in more deprived areas. The gap is narrowing but real. Knowing this isn't to frighten you — it's so you feel entitled to be listened to, to ask questions, and to escalate if you're not being heard.

Perimenopause & menopause education

Perimenopause can start in your 40s (sometimes late 30s) and symptoms can begin years before periods stop. You deserve a proper conversation about your options — including HRT — not to be dismissed or told to just cope.

Symptoms often missed

  • Changes to periods, hot flushes and night sweats
  • Low mood, anxiety, brain fog and poor sleep
  • Joint aches, palpitations, and changes to libido
  • Vaginal dryness or recurrent UTIs (very treatable)

What to know going in

  • Diagnosis is usually on symptoms — a blood test isn't needed over 45
  • HRT is the first-line option for most; ask about benefits and risks for you
  • There are non-hormonal options too if HRT isn't right for you
  • You can ask for a dedicated double appointment to cover it properly

Cancer: symptoms, referrals and support

Most symptoms are not cancer, but getting changes checked early gives the best chance of finding problems sooner. If something is worrying you, it is always okay to contact your GP. This is general information, not a diagnosis.

Get urgent help now

Call 999 or go to A&E for severe breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding that will not stop, severe sudden pain, or if someone is very unwell or confused. Call NHS 111 if you are worried and are not sure what to do. If you are having cancer treatment and develop a fever or feel unwell, call your hospital’s emergency number straight away.

Changes worth telling your GP about

  • A new lump or swelling anywhere in the body
  • Unexplained weight loss or constant tiredness
  • Blood when you cough, in your poo, in your urine, or bleeding after the menopause or between periods
  • A cough, hoarse voice or heartburn that lasts 3 weeks or more
  • A change in bowel habit that does not settle
  • Difficulty swallowing, or persistent bloating
  • A mole or skin patch that changes, itches, bleeds or does not heal

This is not a full list. If something is new, unexplained and not going away, get it checked.

If you are told “it’s probably nothing”

  • Say clearly how long it has lasted and how it affects you
  • Ask: “What would make you want to investigate further?”
  • Ask what to watch for and when to come back
  • Ask for your concern to be recorded in your notes
  • If symptoms continue or worsen, go back, and ask for a second opinion

Skin cancer and darker skin

Skin cancer is less common on brown and black skin, but it still happens, and it can be found later. It can appear in places people do not always check, such as the palms, soles of the feet, under the nails, and inside the mouth. Look for a new or changing mark, a dark streak under a nail, or a sore that does not heal, and show it to your GP.

Urgent suspected cancer referral

If your GP is worried, they can make an urgent suspected cancer referral, sometimes still called a “two-week wait”. It does not mean you have cancer. Most people referred this way do not. It means you will be seen and tested more quickly. NHS England has targets to give most people a diagnosis or the all-clear within 28 days of an urgent referral, and to start treatment within 62 days if cancer is found. Targets are not always met, so ask what the expected timeline is.

Questions to ask your GP

  • Are you making an urgent suspected cancer referral, and when should I hear?
  • What tests or scans should I expect?
  • Who should I contact if I have not heard in a week or two?
  • Can you make sure the referral has my correct phone number?

Making sure you do not fall through the gaps

  • Keep your phone on, and check for letters and texts
  • If you have not been contacted when you were told, call your surgery and ask them to chase it
  • Ask for copies of results and for any letter to be copied to you
  • Write down names, dates and what you were told

Screening: take the invitation

NHS screening checks people who have no symptoms. In England this includes breast screening, bowel screening with a home test kit, and cervical screening. The age ranges and how often you are invited can change, so check the current NHS pages. If you have missed an appointment or lost an invitation, you can usually contact your GP surgery or the screening service to rebook. If you have symptoms, do not wait for a screening invitation, speak to your GP.

If you have been told you may have, or do have, cancer

  • Take someone with you if you can, and ask if you can record or write notes.
  • Ask who your key contact or clinical nurse specialist is, and how to reach them.
  • Ask about every option, including the benefits, risks and what happens if you wait or do nothing.
  • You can ask for a second opinion, and for a copy of your treatment plan. See our private care and second opinions page.
  • Ask about fertility, mental health and practical support, and what help is available at home.

Money, work and everyday life

  • In England, people with cancer can claim a medical exemption certificate for free NHS prescriptions. Ask your GP or treatment team for the form.
  • The Equality Act protects people with cancer from discrimination at work from the point of diagnosis, so talk to your employer or HR about reasonable adjustments.
  • Charities such as Macmillan can help with benefits, grants and money worries.
NHS cancer information NHS screening Macmillan Cancer Support Cancer Research UK Breast Cancer Now Maggie’s

Cancer is serious, but help and support are available. If you are struggling to cope, tell your GP or treatment team, and consider calling a free cancer support line through the charities above.

Eczema — how it looks on different skin tones

On brown and black skin, eczema often looks purple, grey, brown or ashen rather than the classic "red" — so it's frequently missed or judged less severe than it really is. The pattern can differ too (often the front of the knees and the outside of the elbows).

Good to know

  • Afterwards skin can go lighter or darker in patches — this is usually temporary and settles once the eczema is controlled; it isn't scarring from the cream
  • Dryness, thickening, warmth and constant scratching matter as much as colour
  • Ask for severity to be judged with your skin tone in mind, not just redness

Advocate for yourself

If a clinician says "it doesn't look that bad," it's fair to say how it feels, how much you're scratching, and how it affects sleep and daily life — and to ask them to look and feel for thickened, inflamed skin, not just colour.

🔁

The steroid-cream loop — get a proper plan

Topical steroids are a normal, effective part of treating a flare — used at the right strength for a short burst (the mildest ones on the face). The problem is being left on repeat steroid prescriptions for months with no review. That's not a plan.

Ask your GP for a clear plan

  • Which strength, where, and for how many days (step-up for flares, step-down as it settles)
  • What to use between flares (moisturiser is the foundation)
  • A review date — not an endless repeat
  • If creams aren't holding it, ask about non-steroid options or a referral

If stopping steroids causes burning/redness

Long-term use of stronger steroids can occasionally lead to topical steroid withdrawal. If you notice this, don't just stop alone — ask your GP or a dermatologist about it and about alternatives. You can use Right to Choose to get an NHS dermatology referral if the local wait is long.

🧴

Everyday care: fragrance-free, without overspending

Moisturisers (emollients) are the foundation — the single most important thing. Use them generously and often, at least twice a day, even when skin looks clear, and within a few minutes of bathing to lock moisture in.

What to look for

  • Fragrance-free, for sensitive/eczema-prone skin
  • Skip perfumed lotions, soaps and bubble baths; use lukewarm (not hot) water and pat dry
  • Patch-test a new cream on a small area first
  • Ointments are greasier but great overnight; lighter creams for daytime

Keep it affordable

  • Buy the big 500g pump tub — far cheaper per gram than small tubes
  • Supermarket / pharmacy own-brand fragrance-free works just as well as premium "eczema" branding
  • If you go through a lot, ask your GP to prescribe it; an NHS Prescription Prepayment Certificate caps your yearly cost
  • Budget options to ask your pharmacist about: emulsifying ointment, Epaderm/Hydromol, Cetraben, Diprobase or QV
💡

Light therapy — the honest version

NHS phototherapy (the evidence-based one)

For moderate-to-severe eczema that creams aren't controlling, the NHS offers phototherapy (narrowband UVB) under dermatology supervision. If you're at that point, ask your GP for a referral — it's a recognised next step, not a last resort.

At-home red / LED light devices

These are marketed heavily for skin, but the evidence for eczema is limited and good devices can be expensive. They're not a replacement for moisturising and a proper plan. If you want to try one, mention it to your GP or dermatologist first, keep up your emollients, and don't overspend chasing a cure.

This is education and navigation, not medical advice or diagnosis. Always confirm with your GP, pharmacist, midwife, health visitor or NHS 111, and for an emergency call 999.

Across the UK

Care in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

Health care is run separately in each UK nation. Some of Kaur Care’s tools, such as the 1-page brief, the SBAR guide and the symptom log, work anywhere. Others describe NHS England. Here is what differs, and where to start.

What is England-only

  • Right to Choose: the right to choose a different provider for a first outpatient appointment is an English NHS right. It may not apply elsewhere in the UK.
  • NHS Pharmacy First (England): the seven or twelve conditions we list. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own pharmacy schemes, with different names and conditions.
  • NHS App, NHS 111 online and eConsult: the exact services and names differ across the UK.
  • Waiting time targets and screening details: set separately in each nation.

What works everywhere: the brief and SBAR, symptom tracking, second-opinion and referral request letters, and the general advocacy guides. Prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

Private care

Thinking about going private? Know the costs first

Private care can mean a faster appointment or a second opinion, but prices vary a lot and hidden extras are common. This page explains what to expect, what to ask, and how to protect yourself. It is general information, not financial or medical advice.

First, check your free NHS options

Before paying, see whether the NHS can get you seen sooner. In England, Right to Choose can let you be referred to a different NHS-funded provider, sometimes with a much shorter wait, at no cost to you. Pharmacy First covers some everyday conditions the same day. Private care does not replace urgent care: for an emergency call 999, and for urgent advice call NHS 111.

Second and third opinions

A second opinion is another clinician looking at your situation. It can reassure you, confirm a plan, or offer options you had not heard about. You can ask for one on the NHS, and you can also pay privately. There is no automatic right to a second NHS opinion, but a GP or specialist will often arrange one if you ask clearly and politely.

When a second opinion helps

  • Before major surgery or a major treatment
  • When a diagnosis feels uncertain or has changed
  • When you have a rare or complex condition
  • When you have been dismissed and symptoms continue

What about a third?

  • Consider one only if two opinions disagree in a way that matters
  • Ask both clinicians to look at each other’s reports first, it is cheaper and clearer
  • Choose a specialist in that exact condition, ideally at a specialist centre
  • Ask first whether it is likely to change the plan

Make the most of an opinion

Bring your records, scans and reports, ask for the opinion in writing, and prepare your questions in advance. A paid opinion is only worth it if the clinician has the full picture.

How to get a private referral

  • Self-pay: some consultants see you without a referral, but most prefer a GP referral letter so they have your history.
  • With private medical insurance: your insurer usually needs a GP referral and must approve the appointment before you go. Ask them what is covered, and check for exclusions on existing conditions and ongoing care.
  • Asking your NHS GP: GPs can usually write a referral letter to a private specialist. Some surgeries charge for non-NHS paperwork, so ask beforehand.
  • Mixing private and NHS: you can use private care for a consultation or a scan and still use the NHS for treatment, but your GP is not required to prescribe medicines or repeat tests a private clinician recommends. Ask early how follow-up will work.

Questions to ask before you book

The money

  • Can I have a written, itemised quote?
  • What is included: consultation, tests, scans, reports, follow-ups?
  • Are the hospital, consultant, anaesthetist and radiologist billed separately?
  • What happens, and what does it cost, if there are complications?
  • Is there a fixed-price package?

The quality and safety

  • Is the clinic or hospital registered with the CQC (in England)?
  • Is the doctor on the GMC specialist register for this condition?
  • How many of these procedures do they do each year?
  • Who looks after me if something goes wrong, and where?
  • Will my records be sent to my GP?
Check a service on the CQC sitePHIN: private hospital and consultant informationGMC: check a doctor is registered

Keeping the cost down

  • Shop around: get two or three quotes. Prices for the same scan can differ a lot.
  • Pay for the opinion, not everything: a single private consultation, then continuing on the NHS, can cost far less than a full private pathway.
  • Try video appointments where an examination is not needed.
  • Ask for a package price for tests, scans and follow-up together.
  • Bring your existing results so scans and blood tests are not repeated.
  • Watch for upselling: ask what each test is for and whether it will change the plan.
  • Check before using insurance or financing, and read the terms on interest and exclusions.

Be careful

  • Private does not automatically mean better. Check the clinician’s registration and experience, not just the price or the waiting time.
  • Be cautious of clinics that push expensive packages, quick decisions, or treatments you do not understand.
  • Never delay urgent care to arrange something private. Call 999 for emergencies and NHS 111 for urgent advice.

A simple way to be heard

Tell your story with SBAR

Clinicians hand over information to each other using four steps called SBAR. You can borrow the same shape so your story lands clearly and quickly, even in a short slot.

S — Situation

In one line, what you're here about today.

B — Background

When it started, what led up to it, and what you've already tried.

A — Assessment

How it affects your daily life — work, school, sleep, mood.

R — Request

What you're hoping for: a test, a referral, a treatment, or a clear plan.

For example

S: "I've had headaches most days for two months." B: "They started after a bad bout of stress; paracetamol barely touches them." A: "I'm missing work and can't focus by the afternoon." R: "I'd like to understand what's causing them and whether I need a scan or a referral."

Holistic wellness

Gentle habits that support you alongside care

Small, kind routines can help you feel steadier while you wait for or recover from NHS care. None of this is a treatment — think of it as support around the edges.

🌙

Sleep & rest

A consistent wind-down and regular sleep and wake times. Protecting rest is not indulgent; it's part of healing.

⚖️

Pacing & energy

Break the day into gentler chunks and plan rest before you need it. Pacing helps with fatigue and flares.

🚶‍♀️

Gentle movement

Short walks, stretching or mobility work within your limits, and daylight when you can get it.

🥗

Nourishment

Regular meals, fibre and hydration. Notice foods that seem to settle or unsettle you, without strict rules.

🫶

Stress routines

A few minutes of breathing, journalling or time outdoors. Lowering the background hum helps your body cope.

📓

Track what helps

Jot supplements, routines and how you feel in the free 5-day log, so you can share patterns with your GP.

A calm boundary. These habits support wellbeing but do not replace NHS care, diagnosis or treatment, and nothing here cures a condition. If symptoms are new, worsening or worrying, speak to your pharmacist or GP — and for anything serious, call 999 or NHS 111.

Good to know

Your questions, answered

Is Kaur Care free?

Yes. Checking your NHS Pharmacy First eligibility, your Right to Choose rights and the official NHS and CQC directories is completely free, with no sign-up. The only paid extra is the optional document generators (the referral letter and GP brief), which save you the writing.

Is this an official NHS service?

No. Kaur Care is an independent navigation tool that helps you understand and reach real NHS services faster. We point you to official NHS and CQC pages; we are not the NHS and we do not provide treatment.

Is this medical advice?

No. Kaur Care helps you navigate to the right care, but it does not diagnose, prescribe or replace a consultation. Always confirm eligibility with your pharmacy, GP or NHS 111, and for anything serious call 999 or 111.

What happens to my health information?

The referral letter and symptom brief are generated entirely in your browser. The details you type never reach our servers and are never stored by us. See our privacy note for the full picture.

Do I need to create an account?

No. You can search and see all your NHS options without signing up. You only enter an email if you choose to buy the optional document pass.

Does it work across the UK?

The NHS Pharmacy First and Right to Choose pathways apply in England. The self-care guidance and the appointment-prep tools are useful wherever you are in the UK. Always confirm local availability with your pharmacy or GP.
