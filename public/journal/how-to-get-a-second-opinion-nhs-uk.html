How to Get a Second Opinion on the NHS (and When to Consider Going Private)

5 min read · The Kaur Care Journal

If you are unsure about a diagnosis, a treatment plan, or being told nothing more can be done, asking for a second opinion is a normal, reasonable thing to do. Most clinicians understand why patients want one. Here is how to ask, what to expect, and when paying privately is an option.

Is there a right to a second opinion?

There is no absolute legal right to a second opinion on the NHS, but a request is commonly agreed when there is a reasonable case for it, particularly for serious or complex conditions. Having a clear reason makes it much more likely. Rules differ across the UK, so check your nation's guidance, see care across the UK.

Good reasons to ask

  • You have a serious or life-changing diagnosis, or a major treatment is being proposed.
  • Your symptoms continue and you have not been given a clear explanation.
  • You were told nothing more can be done and you would like another view on the options.
  • You have lost confidence in the plan, or in the communication around it.

How to ask

  1. Ask the clinician you are seeing, your GP or hospital consultant. Say it without blame: "I would like to ask for a second opinion, to be sure I understand all my options."
  2. Explain your reason briefly, and what you hope a second opinion would help with.
  3. Ask them to make the referral, and to say who they would suggest, or whether you can name a specialist or centre.
  4. Get it in writing if you can, and ask for your records and results to be shared with the second doctor.

If the answer is no

You can ask what the reasons are and for them to be recorded in your notes. You can also raise it with the practice manager or the hospital's Patient Advice and Liaison Service (PALS), and our guide to making a complaint covers the formal route if you need it.

Going private for a second opinion

You can pay for a second opinion from a private consultant without your GP's agreement, though many specialists will want a GP or consultant letter and your records, so ask for those. A single private consultation often costs in the low hundreds of pounds, and scans or tests are extra, so ask for a full price before you book. Our private care guide explains costs and what to ask. You can often return to the NHS afterwards with the report in hand.

Make the most of it

Bring a one-page summary, your questions, copies of results and a list of medicines. Ask the second doctor to explain what is the same as, and different from, the first view. Having a clear page in front of you, like the appointment brief, keeps the visit focused.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you are worried about your safety or your symptoms are getting worse quickly, call NHS 111 or 999.
