Babies, pregnancy & menopause

Care and knowledge for every stage

Clear, calm guidance for new babies, pregnancy and the menopause years — with honest information about where care can fall short, so you can advocate early.

👶

Babies & newborns

Your health visitor and your baby's red book (personal child health record) are there for you between GP visits. Trust your instincts — you know your baby best.

Good to know

  • Keep feeding, nappies and temperature noted — patterns help clinicians
  • Follow safer-sleep guidance to reduce the risk of SIDS
  • Keep up with the NHS vaccination schedule and development reviews
  • It's always okay to ask your health visitor or ring 111 for reassurance

When to get urgent help

Call 999 for a baby who is hard to wake, has trouble breathing, a rash that doesn't fade under a glass, a fit/seizure, or a high-pitched or weak cry. Trust your gut — you will not be wasting anyone's time.

NHS Start for Life — baby NHS baby health A–Z Safer sleep (Lullaby Trust) Vaccination schedule
🤰

Pregnancy & maternal health equity

You can usually self-refer to maternity services as soon as you know you're pregnant — you don't have to wait for a GP. Booking by 10 weeks means earlier checks and support.

Know the red flags — speak up straight away

  • A change in your baby's usual movements (don't wait — ring your maternity unit any time, day or night)
  • A bad headache, vision changes or sudden swelling (possible pre-eclampsia)
  • Bleeding, severe pain, or feeling feverish and unwell
  • A strong sense that something is wrong — say so clearly and ask to be checked

Honest words on inequality

Maternity care should be safe for everyone, of every background. But UK data (MBRRACE-UK) shows Black women have been around two to three times more likely to die in pregnancy or soon after than white women, with raised risks for some Asian and ethnic-minority women and in more deprived areas. The gap is narrowing but real. Knowing this isn't to frighten you — it's so you feel entitled to be listened to, to ask questions, and to escalate if you're not being heard.

NHS pregnancy guide Five X More (Black maternal health) Birthrights (your rights in birth) Tommy's (pregnancy support) MBRRACE-UK (the evidence)

Perimenopause & menopause education

Perimenopause can start in your 40s (sometimes late 30s) and symptoms can begin years before periods stop. You deserve a proper conversation about your options — including HRT — not to be dismissed or told to just cope.

Symptoms often missed

  • Changes to periods, hot flushes and night sweats
  • Low mood, anxiety, brain fog and poor sleep
  • Joint aches, palpitations, and changes to libido
  • Vaginal dryness or recurrent UTIs (very treatable)

What to know going in

  • Diagnosis is usually on symptoms — a blood test isn't needed over 45
  • HRT is the first-line option for most; ask about benefits and risks for you
  • There are non-hormonal options too if HRT isn't right for you
  • You can ask for a dedicated double appointment to cover it properly
NHS menopause NICE guidance (NG23) Women's Health Concern British Menopause Society Daisy Network (early menopause)

This is education and navigation, not medical advice or diagnosis. Always confirm with your GP, midwife, health visitor or NHS 111, and for an emergency call 999.

Eczema & skin

Eczema care that actually fits your skin

Eczema is common and manageable — but it looks different on different skin tones, and too many people get stuck on repeat steroid creams without a real plan. Here's how to get seen properly and care for your skin day to day, affordably.

🧑🏽‍🤝‍🧑🏿

How eczema looks on different skin tones

On brown and black skin, eczema often looks purple, grey, brown or ashen rather than the classic "red" — so it's frequently missed or judged less severe than it really is. The pattern can differ too (often the front of the knees and the outside of the elbows).

Good to know

  • Afterwards skin can go lighter or darker in patches — this is usually temporary and settles once the eczema is controlled; it isn't scarring from the cream
  • Dryness, thickening, warmth and constant scratching matter as much as colour
  • Ask for severity to be judged with your skin tone in mind, not just redness

Advocate for yourself

If a clinician says "it doesn't look that bad," it's fair to say how it feels, how much you're scratching, and how it affects sleep and daily life — and to ask them to look and feel for thickened, inflamed skin, not just colour.

Skin pigmentation & eczema (National Eczema Society) NHS atopic eczema
🔁

The steroid-cream loop — get a proper plan

Topical steroids are a normal, effective part of treating a flare — used at the right strength for a short burst (the mildest ones on the face). The problem is being left on repeat steroid prescriptions for months with no review. That's not a plan.

Ask your GP for a clear plan

  • Which strength, where, and for how many days (step-up for flares, step-down as it settles)
  • What to use between flares (moisturiser is the foundation)
  • A review date — not an endless repeat
  • If creams aren't holding it, ask about non-steroid options or a referral

If stopping steroids causes burning/redness

Long-term use of stronger steroids can occasionally lead to topical steroid withdrawal. If you notice this, don't just stop alone — ask your GP or a dermatologist about it and about alternatives. You can use Right to Choose to get an NHS dermatology referral if the local wait is long.

🧴

Everyday care: fragrance-free, without overspending

Moisturisers (emollients) are the foundation — the single most important thing. Use them generously and often, at least twice a day, even when skin looks clear, and within a few minutes of bathing to lock moisture in.

What to look for

  • Fragrance-free, for sensitive/eczema-prone skin
  • Skip perfumed lotions, soaps and bubble baths; use lukewarm (not hot) water and pat dry
  • Patch-test a new cream on a small area first
  • Ointments are greasier but great overnight; lighter creams for daytime

Keep it affordable

  • Buy the big 500g pump tub — far cheaper per gram than small tubes
  • Supermarket / pharmacy own-brand fragrance-free works just as well as premium "eczema" branding
  • If you go through a lot, ask your GP to prescribe it; an NHS Prescription Prepayment Certificate caps your yearly cost
  • Budget options to ask your pharmacist about: emulsifying ointment, Epaderm/Hydromol, Cetraben, Diprobase or QV
💡

Light therapy — the honest version

NHS phototherapy (the evidence-based one)

For moderate-to-severe eczema that creams aren't controlling, the NHS offers phototherapy (narrowband UVB) under dermatology supervision. If you're at that point, ask your GP for a referral — it's a recognised next step, not a last resort.

At-home red / LED light devices

These are marketed heavily for skin, but the evidence for eczema is limited and good devices can be expensive. They're not a replacement for moisturising and a proper plan. If you want to try one, mention it to your GP or dermatologist first, keep up your emollients, and don't overspend chasing a cure.

This is education and navigation, not medical advice or diagnosis. Confirm anything about treatments with your GP, pharmacist or dermatologist, and for a severe skin infection (hot, spreading, you feel unwell) seek urgent care.

Appointment prep playbook

Make every minute of your appointment count

A short, calm plan for before, during and after. You deserve to be heard the first time.

  • 1.Write your top three concerns in priority order, so the most important one is never missed.
  • 2.Note when it started, how often it happens, and what makes it better or worse.
  • 3.List what you've already tried — medicines, doses and whether they helped.
  • 4.Bring a few days of notes. The free 5-day symptom & flare log in the prep tools does this for you.
  • 5.Decide the one question you most need answered before you leave.
  • •Lead with your headline: "I'm here about X, and it's affecting Y."
  • •Give your top concern first, in case the time runs out.
  • •Hand over your 1-page brief or log so nothing is forgotten under pressure.
  • •Ask plainly: "What might be causing this, and what's the plan?"
  • •Before you leave, confirm next steps and what should prompt you to come back sooner.
  • •Ask for your concerns and decisions to be written in your notes.
  • •It is okay to ask for a second opinion if something doesn't feel resolved.
  • •Request copies of results and any referral letters for your own records.
  • •For a long wait, use your Right to Choose (see the results above) to ask for a quicker NHS-funded provider.
  • •Book a follow-up and ask what change in symptoms should trigger an earlier review.

A simple way to be heard

Tell your story with SBAR

Clinicians hand over information to each other using four steps called SBAR. You can borrow the same shape so your story lands clearly and quickly, even in a short slot.

S — Situation

In one line, what you're here about today.

B — Background

When it started, what led up to it, and what you've already tried.

A — Assessment

How it affects your daily life — work, school, sleep, mood.

R — Request

What you're hoping for: a test, a referral, a treatment, or a clear plan.

For example

S: "I've had headaches most days for two months." B: "They started after a bad bout of stress; paracetamol barely touches them." A: "I'm missing work and can't focus by the afternoon." R: "I'd like to understand what's causing them and whether I need a scan or a referral."

Prep checklists by situation

Women's health

  • Cycle dates and length over the last few months
  • Pain score (0–10), where it is and how it tracks with your cycle
  • Bleeding heaviness (flooding, clots, products per hour)
  • Impact on work, sleep, energy and mood

Chronic illness & flares

  • How often flares happen and what seems to trigger them
  • Fatigue and pain scores on a typical and a bad day
  • Current medicines, doses and any side effects
  • What helps, and what you've had to stop doing

ADHD & autism

  • Specific examples at home, work or school
  • Traits noticed since childhood
  • How it affects focus, relationships and daily function
  • Any screening questionnaires you've completed

Parents & children

  • Temperature, feeding and wet/dirty nappies
  • How long symptoms have lasted and whether they're improving
  • A clear photo of any rash (press a glass against it to check)
  • Any red flags: drowsiness, breathing trouble, a stiff neck

Holistic wellness

Gentle habits that support you alongside care

Small, kind routines can help you feel steadier while you wait for or recover from NHS care. None of this is a treatment — think of it as support around the edges.

🌙

Sleep & rest

A consistent wind-down and regular sleep and wake times. Protecting rest is not indulgent; it's part of healing.

⚖️

Pacing & energy

Break the day into gentler chunks and plan rest before you need it. Pacing helps with fatigue and flares.

🚶‍♀️

Gentle movement

Short walks, stretching or mobility work within your limits, and daylight when you can get it.

🥗

Nourishment

Regular meals, fibre and hydration. Notice foods that seem to settle or unsettle you, without strict rules.

🫶

Stress routines

A few minutes of breathing, journalling or time outdoors. Lowering the background hum helps your body cope.

📓

Track what helps

Jot supplements, routines and how you feel in the free 5-day log, so you can share patterns with your GP.

A calm boundary. These habits support wellbeing but do not replace NHS care, diagnosis or treatment, and nothing here cures a condition. If symptoms are new, worsening or worrying, speak to your pharmacist or GP — and for anything serious, call 999 or NHS 111.

Good to know

Your questions, answered

Is Kaur Care free?

Yes. Checking your NHS Pharmacy First eligibility, your Right to Choose rights and the official NHS and CQC directories is completely free, with no sign-up. The only paid extra is the optional document generators (the referral letter and GP brief), which save you the writing.

Is this an official NHS service?

No. Kaur Care is an independent navigation tool that helps you understand and reach real NHS services faster. We point you to official NHS and CQC pages; we are not the NHS and we do not provide treatment.

Is this medical advice?

No. Kaur Care helps you navigate to the right care, but it does not diagnose, prescribe or replace a consultation. Always confirm eligibility with your pharmacy, GP or NHS 111, and for anything serious call 999 or 111.

What happens to my health information?

The referral letter and symptom brief are generated entirely in your browser. The details you type never reach our servers and are never stored by us. See our privacy note for the full picture.

Do I need to create an account?

No. You can search and see all your NHS options without signing up. You only enter an email if you choose to buy the optional document pass.

Does it work across the UK?

The NHS Pharmacy First and Right to Choose pathways apply in England. The self-care guidance and the appointment-prep tools are useful wherever you are in the UK. Always confirm local availability with your pharmacy or GP.
