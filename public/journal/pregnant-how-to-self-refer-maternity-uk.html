Just Found Out You're Pregnant? How to Self-Refer for NHS Maternity Care

5 min read · The Kaur Care Journal

You have taken the test and it is positive. What now? In most parts of England you do not need to see your GP first. You can usually refer yourself directly to your local NHS maternity service, which is often the quickest way to get started.

How to self-refer

Most NHS trusts have a self-referral form on their website, usually under "maternity" or "pregnancy". Search for your local trust's maternity self-referral, or ask your GP surgery for the route in your area. You will be asked for details such as your name, date of birth, address, GP and your estimated due date, and you will usually be contacted to arrange your first midwife appointment.

When to book

NHS guidance is to contact your midwife or GP as soon as you know you are pregnant, ideally so that your booking appointment happens by about 10 weeks. This is when you will be asked about your health history and offered tests and information on screening and care. If you have an existing health condition, take medicines regularly, or have had complications in a previous pregnancy, tell the service straight away, as you may need extra support. Do not stop prescribed medicines without advice.

Red flags: do not wait

Contact your maternity unit or call NHS 111, and call 999 in an emergency, if you notice:

  • A change in your baby's usual movements. Contact your maternity unit straight away, day or night. Do not wait until the next day.
  • A severe headache, vision problems, pain just below the ribs, or sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet, which can be signs of pre-eclampsia.
  • Vaginal bleeding, severe tummy pain or fluid leaking.
  • Feeling very unwell, or a strong sense that something is wrong. Say so clearly and ask to be checked.

Speaking up and feeling heard

Maternity care should be safe and respectful for everyone. UK data from MBRRACE-UK shows that outcomes are not equal: in recent reports Black women have been around two to three times more likely to die during or soon after pregnancy than white women, and risks are also higher in more deprived areas, although the gap has narrowed in the latest figures. Knowing this is not meant to frighten you. It is a reminder that you are entitled to be listened to, to ask questions and to escalate if you do not feel heard.

Practical steps: write down your questions, bring someone with you if you can, ask for things to be explained again, and ask for your concerns to be recorded in your notes. Organisations such as Five X More, Birthrights and Tommy's offer information and support.
