Eczema Moisturisers on a Budget: Fragrance-Free Emollients and the Steroid Cream Loop

6 min read · The Kaur Care Journal

If you live with eczema, you have probably been through lots of creams. The good news is that the most important treatment, moisturising, does not need to be expensive or fancy. Here is how to do it well and affordably, and how to avoid getting stuck on repeat steroid prescriptions with no plan.

Emollients are the foundation

Emollients are moisturisers designed for dry, eczema-prone skin. NHS guidance recommends using them regularly, at least twice a day, even when your skin looks clear. Apply generously, and within a few minutes of bathing to lock in moisture. Smooth them on in the direction of hair growth rather than rubbing in hard.

Choosing a fragrance-free one

  • Choose fragrance-free products, as perfume can irritate sensitive skin.
  • Thicker ointments are very moisturising, good for very dry skin and overnight, but greasy.
  • Lighter creams and lotions can be easier for daytime and warmer weather.
  • Avoid perfumed soaps, bubble baths and hot water. Use lukewarm water and pat dry.
  • Patch-test a new product on a small area first.

Your pharmacist can help you choose, and it is normal to try more than one to find what suits you.

How to keep the cost down

  • Buy in bulk. A large pump tub is usually far cheaper per gram than small tubes.
  • Try supermarket and pharmacy own-brand fragrance-free options. Plain emollients work on the same principle regardless of branding.
  • Ask for a prescription if you use a lot. Prescriptions are free for children under 16 and for people in other exempt groups in England. Otherwise, check whether a Prescription Prepayment Certificate would save you money, and look up current prices and eligibility on the NHS Business Services Authority website.
  • Avoid expensive products with big claims. A higher price does not mean better results for eczema.

The steroid cream loop

Topical steroids are a normal, effective part of treating eczema flares, usually at the lowest strength that works, for the shortest time, and the mildest ones on the face and delicate skin. The problem some people face is being left on repeat prescriptions for months or years with no review. Ask your GP for a clear plan:

  • Which strength, on which areas, and for how many days.
  • What to use between flares.
  • When you will be reviewed, rather than an open-ended repeat.
  • What the next step is if creams are not holding it, such as other treatments or a referral.

If you have used strong steroids for a long time and notice burning, redness or worsening skin after stopping, do not just carry on or stop alone, speak to your GP or a dermatologist. If the wait to see one is long, you may be able to use your Right to Choose for an NHS dermatology referral.

Light therapy: what the evidence says

For moderate to severe eczema that is not controlled by creams, the NHS can offer phototherapy under dermatology supervision, so ask your GP whether you are a candidate. Home red or LED light devices are heavily marketed, but the evidence for treating eczema is limited. If you are considering one, talk to your GP or dermatologist first and keep up your emollients rather than relying on a device.
